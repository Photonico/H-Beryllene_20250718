                                               Abstract
    Two-dimensional elemental materials provide a unique platform for exploring electronic, optical,
and topological phenomena arising from reduced dimensionality and modified bonding environ-
ments. Here, using density functional theory calculations, we investigate the structural stability,
electronic structure, optical response, and topological characteristics of pristine and hydrogen-
functionalized beryllene systems, including monolayer α-beryllene, bilayer β-beryllene, and a newly
identified cubic trilayer phase, together with bulk reference phases. In addition to confirming the
stability of previously proposed α- and β-beryllene phases, we predict a dynamically stable cubic
trilayer beryllene phase with distinct electronic and optical characteristics. Hydrogen functional-
ization substantially modifies the structural and electronic properties, yielding stable hydrogen-
terminated configurations and driving α-beryllene into a wide-gap semiconducting state, while
preserving metallic behaviour in hydrogen-functionalized β-based structures. The calculated dielec-
tric response reveals strong anisotropy and pronounced low-dimensional optical effects, including
enhanced visible-to-ultraviolet absorption, modified plasmonic behaviour, and direction-dependent
dielectric responses associated with collective electronic excitations. Furthermore, parity analysis of
the spin-orbit-coupled electronic states demonstrates that, beyond previously reported α-beryllene,
the cubic trilayer phase also exhibits a non-trivial Z2 topological invariant, indicating the emer-
gence of topologically protected electronic states in a light-element system. These results highlight
beryllene as an unusual elemental two-dimensional platform combining metallicity, tunable opti-
cal properties, and non-trivial topology, with potential relevance for future nanoscale electronic,
photonic, and quantum technologies.

I.    INTRODUCTION


     Two-dimensional materials have reshaped modern materials science by enabling access
to electronic, thermal, and mechanical properties that differ substantially from those of
their bulk counterparts. Since the isolation of graphene, reduced dimensionality has been
shown to give rise to Dirac fermions, enhanced carrier mobility, strong many-body inter-
actions, tuneable band structures, and anomalous phonon transport [1, 2]. These effects
support rapid progress in nanoelectronics, optoelectronics, sensing, thermal management,
and quantum technologies.
     Beyond graphene, the discovery and prediction of atomically thin materials has expanded
to include transition-metal dichalcogenides, hexagonal boron nitride, black phosphorus, and
elemental two-dimensional materials, commonly referred to as Xenes [3, 4]. Elemental two-
dimensional materials are particularly attractive because their chemically simple nature
allows intrinsic structure-property relationships to be studied without the complexity intro-
duced by multicomponent bonding. However, most experimentally realized Xenes are com-
posed of relatively heavy elements, where strong spin-orbit coupling, substrate effects, or
structural buckling dominate the physics and may obscure low-energy electronic behaviour.
     In contrast, beryllium represents an extreme and largely unexplored limit within the
family of elemental two-dimensional materials. As the lightest alkaline-earth metal, bulk
beryllium exhibits a unique combination of low density, high elastic modulus, high ther-
mal conductivity, and transparency to X-rays and neutrons. These properties have led to
its use in aerospace, nuclear, and optical applications [5]. Extending elemental beryllium
into the two-dimensional limit raises the prospect of ultralight metallic layers with uncon-
ventional electronic and thermal properties governed by reduced dimensionality and strong
confinement.
     First-principles studies predicted that atomically thin beryllium could exist in metastable
hexagonal or stable two-layer hexagonal lattice configurations [6, 7]. However, for many
years, two-dimensional beryllium remained a theoretical construct due to experimental chal-
lenges associated with synthesis and handling. This situation changed recently with the
experimental realization of two-dimensional beryllium, termed beryllene. Chahal et al. re-
ported the first experimental synthesis and characterization of beryllene using a sonochem-
ical exfoliation approach starting from elemental beryllium powder [8]. High-resolution

                                               2
transmission electron microscopy revealed atomically thin sheets with multiple structural
motifs, including hexagonal and square-like lattice geometries. Raman spectroscopy and
electron diffraction confirmed crystallinity, while electrical measurements demonstrated in-
trinsic metallic conductivity.
   Subsequent theoretical investigations have uncovered striking thermal transport be-
haviour in hexagonal monolayer beryllene. Using first-principles phonon calculations com-
bined with the Boltzmann transport equation, Chowdhury and Mogurampelly predicted an
exceptionally high in-plane lattice thermal conductivity of approximately 270 W m−1 K−1
at room temperature, which exceeds that of bulk beryllium [9]. This enhancement was
attributed to suppressed phonon-phonon scattering and the dominant contribution of long-
wavelength flexural phonons, a feature rarely observed in atomically thin metallic systems.
These results suggest that two-dimensional beryllium could serve as an efficient heat-
spreading material in nanoscale electronic architectures.
   Beyond thermal properties, the electronic structure of beryllene has been predicted to
host non-trivial quantum features. Several density functional theory studies have identified
Dirac-like band crossings near the Fermi level in hexagonal monolayer and bilayer beryl-
lene, usually denoted as α- and β-beryllene, arising from symmetry-protected s-p orbital
hybridization [10]. Electron-phonon coupling calculations further suggest that these phases
may support phonon-mediated superconductivity with critical temperatures on the order of
10 K. The coexistence of Dirac fermions and superconductivity in an ultralight elemental
monolayer distinguishes beryllene from both graphene and heavier Xenes, and highlights its
potential as a platform for low-dimensional quantum phenomena.
   The possibility of topological behaviour in two-dimensional beryllium has also been ex-
plored from a more fundamental perspective. Derriche et al. demonstrated that light-
element lattices dominated by charge-based interactions can host topologically protected
electronic states even in the absence of strong spin-orbit coupling [11]. These findings sug-
gest that beryllene may realize topological phases driven mainly by lattice symmetry and
orbital physics, offering an alternative route to low-dissipation electronic states without re-
lying on heavy elements. While experimental studies of beryllene remain limited, earlier
work on ultrathin beryllium films and nanostructures has revealed unconventional quantum
transport behaviour, including strong disorder effects and superconducting correlations at
low temperatures [12].

                                             3
   Two-dimensional topological materials are considered promising for quantum and elec-
tronic technologies because their non-trivial band topology gives rise to robust, symmetry-
protected edge states that resist disorder and backscattering [13]. Recent density functional
theory studies have shown that simple elemental two-dimensional systems can host switch-
able or adsorbate-induced topological behaviour. For example, interlayer sliding in bilayer
elemental bismuth was predicted to switch the system between topologically trivial and non-
trivial phases [14]. Similarly, hydrogenation and strain can tune monolayer arsenene into a
quantum spin Hall state with a sizable band gap [15]. These studies indicate that structural
control and adsorbate functionalization can provide practical routes to topological phases
in elemental two-dimensional materials.

   Although two-dimensional elemental beryllium has recently been realized experimentally
and shown to exhibit unusual electronic and thermal properties, its interaction with hydrogen
has received much less attention. First-principles investigations of hydrogen adsorption
on low-index hexagonal close-packed beryllium surfaces, particularly Be(0001), show that
hydrogen binds preferentially to high-symmetry adsorption sites with moderate binding
energies and induces appreciable local lattice relaxation [16]. These results indicate that
beryllium-hydrogen interactions occupy an intermediate regime between weak physisorption
and strong ionic hydride formation. In the two-dimensional limit, reduced coordination
and enhanced lattice flexibility are expected to modify this balance, potentially stabilizing
adsorption geometries or electronic reconstructions that are inaccessible on bulk surfaces.
Using global structure search and density functional theory calculations, two stable beryllium
hydride monolayers were predicted with multicentre beryllium-hydrogen bonding motifs and
wide band gaps [17]. Although such systems correspond to compound formation rather
than surface termination, they demonstrate that hydrogen can strongly reshape bonding
and electronic behaviour in low-dimensional beryllium-based systems.

   In this work, we use density functional theory calculations to investigate the atomic, elec-
tronic, optical, and topological properties of pristine and hydrogen-functionalized beryllene
systems. The results identify stable hydrogen-functionalized structures and a cubic tri-
layer geometry with non-trivial Z2 topology. The optical properties demonstrate direction-
dependent and dimensionality-dependent response, together with plasmon-related features
and visible-to-ultraviolet absorption.

II.     COMPUTATIONAL METHODS


      The density functional theory calculations are performed using the Vienna Ab initio
Simulation Package, VASP [18–21]. The projector augmented wave method is used to de-
scribe the electron-ion interactions [22]. The generalized gradient approximation of Perdew–
Burke–Ernzerhof is employed for the exchange-correlation functional [23], along with the D3
dispersion correction with Becke–Johnson damping to account for long-range van der Waals
interactions [24–26].
      For bulk hexagonal close-packed beryllium and bulk body-centred cubic beryllium, a Γ-
centered k-point mesh of 27 × 27 × 27 is used for the total energy and structural relaxation
calculations. A 105 × 105 × 105 mesh is used for the calculation of the complex dielectric
function. A plane-wave energy cutoff of 600 eV is used for all calculations except the topolog-
ical calculations described below. The electronic convergence criterion is set to 1 × 10−6 eV,

tures. For all two-dimensional systems, a 105 × 105 × 1 mesh is used for the calculation of
the complex dielectric function. Convergence tests for the cohesive and total energies, as

    Among the pristine two-dimensional phases, α-beryllene and β-beryllene correspond to
the previously proposed hexagonal monolayer and bilayer forms of beryllene. α-beryllene
forms a planar hexagonal network, where each beryllium atom bonds to six neighbouring
beryllium atoms. β-beryllene can be regarded as a bilayer form of α-beryllene, where beryl-
lium atoms in the upper layer sit above hollow sites of the lower layer. In addition to these
known phases, we also identify a cubic trilayer beryllene structure. This structure adopts an
AB stacking arrangement, where the beryllium atoms in the central layer are located at the
hollow sites of the upper and lower layers. It therefore provides a distinct structural motif
within the same elemental system.
    The cohesive energies of α-beryllene, β-beryllene, and cubic trilayer beryllene are 0.79 eV,
0.44 eV, and 0.43 eV per atom less favourable than that of bulk hcp beryllium, respectively.
Compared with bulk hcp beryllium, the in-plane lattice constants of α-beryllene and β-
beryllene are reduced by 6.0 % and 6.4 %, respectively. The interlayer beryllium–beryllium
distance in β-beryllene is 6.5 % larger than that in bulk hcp beryllium. For cubic trilayer
beryllene, the in-plane lattice constant is 0.8 % larger than that of bulk bcc beryllium,

                                                      9
whereas the interlayer distance is reduced by 17 %.
    We also tested two-layer and four-layer cubic beryllene structures, as well as graphene-
like beryllium layers from one to four layers. These structures were found to be dynamically
unstable from the phonon dispersion calculations, and are therefore not discussed further.

    We then consider hydrogen functionalization of the two-dimensional phases. For α-
beryllene, we consider the double-sided hydrogen-terminated configuration. For β-beryllene,
both single-sided and double-sided hydrogen-terminated configurations are considered.
These hydrogen-functionalized structures preserve the underlying beryllium framework but
modify the local bonding environment around the surface beryllium atoms. As a result,
hydrogen adsorption is expected to affect the energetic stability, electronic structure, and
optical response of the corresponding beryllene phases.

   B.   Dynamical stability and phonon dispersion


   We then evaluate the dynamical stability of the beryllium structures using phonon disper-
sion calculations. The phonon dispersion curves are calculated using finite differences with
5 × 5 supercells. The bulk hcp and bulk bcc structures are dynamically stable, as are the
pristine α-beryllene and β-beryllene structures, as shown in figure S13 of the Supplementary
Information. This result is consistent with previous reports on α- and β-beryllene.
   Hydrogen functionalization can modify the structural and electronic properties of a two-
dimensional material by changing the local bonding environment. We therefore examine
hydrogen adsorption on the beryllene structures. Among the hydrogen adsorption configu-
rations considered, the structures that satisfy both energetic and dynamical stability crite-
ria are double-sided hydrogen-terminated α-beryllene, single-sided hydrogen-terminated β-
beryllene, and double-sided hydrogen-terminated β-beryllene. For brevity, we denote these
structures as 2H-α-beryllene, 1H-β-beryllene, and 2H-β-beryllene, respectively.
   The phonon dispersion curves of cubic trilayer beryllene and the hydrogen-functionalized
structures are shown in figure 4. No significant imaginary frequencies appear in these spectra,
which indicates that cubic trilayer beryllene, 2H-α-beryllene, 1H-β-beryllene, and 2H-β-
beryllene are dynamically stable. The Brillouin zones used for these phonon paths are
shown in figure S14 of the Supplementary Information.
   We further examine the finite-temperature stability of the hydrogen-functionalized struc-
tures using ab initio molecular dynamics simulations. The AIMD trajectories and the cor-
responding atomic structures are shown in figure 5. For all three hydrogen-functionalized
structures, the total energy fluctuates around a stable average value, and the temperature
remains close to the target range during the simulation. The corresponding atomic snap-
shots preserve the beryllene frameworks without structural collapse. These results support
the finite-temperature stability of the hydrogen-functionalized phases.

                            C.     Electronic properties


                            We now examine the electronic properties of pristine and hydrogen-functionalized beryl-
     lene structures. The band structures of pristine α-beryllene and β-beryllene are shown in
     figure S15 of the Supplementary Information, and agree well with previous calculations [10].
     The band structures of cubic trilayer beryllene and the hydrogen-functionalized structures
     are shown in figure 6.

                            The cubic trilayer beryllene structure shows metallic behaviour, with multiple bands
     crossing the Fermi level. The bands near the Fermi level show moderate dispersion, which
     suggests delocalized electronic states and good in-plane conductivity. Around the Γ, M,
     and X points, noticeable band splitting appears, consistent with interlayer coupling in the
     trilayer structure. The coexistence of relatively flat bands and dispersive unoccupied bands
     at higher energies further indicates a mixture of localized and itinerant electronic states.

                                                                                                              13
   Hydrogen functionalization changes the electronic structure differently for the α and β
phases. For 2H-α-beryllene, the band structure shows a wide indirect band gap exceeding
4.0 eV at the PBE level. Since PBE generally underestimates band gaps, the actual gap is
expected to be larger. In contrast, both 1H-β-beryllene and 2H-β-beryllene remain metallic,
with bands crossing the Fermi level. These results show that hydrogen functionalization
opens a large gap in α-beryllene, whereas the hydrogen-functionalized β-based structures
preserve their metallic character.

We also apply the same analysis to cubic trilayer beryllene.

Gaussian smearing of 0.02 eV is used. Spin-orbit coupling is included in the band-structure
and parity calculations. The parity eigenvalues of the spinor wave functions are calculated
using irvsp [27].

   E.   Optical properties


   We now examine the optical properties of the pristine and hydrogen-functionalized beryl-
lene structures, together with the bulk hcp and bulk bcc beryllium reference phases. The
dielectric function, absorption coefficient, refractive index, extinction coefficient, reflectivity,
and energy-loss spectrum are defined in section II. Here, we use these quantities to compare
the direction-dependent optical response of the beryllene structures.



   1.   Pristine α- and β-beryllene


   We first discuss the optical response of pristine α-beryllene and β-beryllene, which then
provides a reference for the bulk, cubic trilayer, and hydrogen-functionalized structures.
The real and imaginary parts of the dielectric function for the in-plane and out-of-plane
directions are shown in figure 9.
   For β-beryllene, the in-plane real part indicates two plasmon excitations at approximately
2.5 eV and 5.3 eV, as well as plasmon-like features above approximately 7 eV. These fea-
tures occur together with relatively small values of the imaginary part. In the out-of-plane
direction, the low-energy plasmon feature is absent. For α-beryllene, the response differs,
and only a higher-energy plasmon-like feature appears around 7 eV to 9 eV. Interband tran-
sitions may coexist with these plasmonic features and form a broad background. When
interband transitions and plasmons occupy the same energy range, they may couple and
produce finite plasmon lifetimes together with interband-transition character.

direction-dependent plasmonic modes.

   2.   Bulk hcp, bulk bcc, and cubic trilayer beryllene

cubic trilayer beryllene. The real and imaginary parts of the dielectric function are shown in

4.5 eV, reaches a minimum around 5.5 eV to 6.0 eV, and then rises smoothly toward zero
around 12 eV. The imaginary part displays a broad low-energy feature up to approximately
5.5 eV, indicating a wide range of allowed interband transitions. It then decreases sharply
around 6 eV, coinciding with the minimum in the real part and indicating a plasmonic optical
response.

          For cubic trilayer beryllene, the most prominent feature is its enhanced visible-region

whereas in the out-of-plane direction the corresponding large peak appears at a lower energy
near 2.5 eV.

   3.   Hydrogen-functionalized beryllene structures


   We now examine the effect of hydrogen adsorption on the optical properties of α-beryllene
and β-beryllene. The real and imaginary parts of the dielectric function for the pristine and
hydrogen-functionalized structures are shown in figure 14.
   For α-beryllene, the pristine structure exhibits a relatively low and slowly varying real
part in the low-energy region below approximately 4 eV, followed by a modest peak near
5 eV to 6 eV. In contrast, 2H-α-beryllene shows a strongly enhanced dielectric response, with
values reaching approximately 60–70 near 5 eV. This increase indicates a strong enhance-
ment of electronic polarizability after hydrogen adsorption. The hydrogen-functionalized
structure also shows a sharp transition from large positive values to negative values around
5.5 eV to 6.0 eV, indicating plasma-like behaviour and a shifted zero crossing relative to pris-
tine α-beryllene. This shift suggests that hydrogen functionalization modifies the electronic
structure by introducing new hybridized states and changing the band dispersion, thereby
strengthening collective electronic oscillations.
   For the imaginary part, the difference is also pronounced. Pristine α-beryllene shows
relatively weak absorption with a modest peak centred around 6 eV. By contrast, 2H-α-
beryllene displays an intense and broadened absorption peak near 5.0 eV to 5.5 eV, together
with additional features at higher photon energies. This enhancement and slight redshift
of the dominant peak indicate that hydrogen adsorption introduces or strengthens optically
allowed transitions, likely through hybridization between hydrogen states and the host elec-
tronic states. The broadening also suggests an increased density of accessible electronic
transitions over a wider energy range. Consequently, 2H-α-beryllene is expected to exhibit
substantially stronger optical absorption in the visible to near-ultraviolet region than pristine
α-beryllene.
   For β-beryllene, the pristine phase shows a moderate low-energy dielectric response, fol-
lowed by a rapid decrease into negative values around 1 eV to 2 eV, and then a recovery with
a broad positive feature near 4 eV to 5 eV. Upon single-sided hydrogen adsorption, the mag-
nitude of the real part is slightly enhanced in the low-energy range, and the spectral features
become shifted and smoothed. The zero crossing shifts to higher energies, around 3 eV to
4 eV, and the oscillatory behaviour becomes less abrupt. This behaviour indicates a redis-
tribution of electronic states and a reduction of sharp interband features. For double-sided

                                               23
  hydrogen adsorption, these effects become stronger. The real part increases over a wider
  energy range and shows a more gradual dispersion, with a delayed and deeper transition into
  negative values near 6 eV to 6.5 eV. This progression suggests that increasing hydrogen cov-
  erage enhances electronic screening and modifies the effective plasma frequency, consistent
  with stronger perturbation of the band structure and increased carrier polarization.
                             For the imaginary part of β-beryllene, the pristine phase shows a sharp and intense low-
  energy absorption peak near 1 eV to 1.5 eV, followed by a weaker and broader feature near
  5 eV. For 1H-β-beryllene, the low-energy peak is reduced in intensity and broadened, while
  a more prominent absorption feature emerges around 3.5 eV to 4.0 eV. This indicates a
  shift of the dominant interband transitions toward higher energies. For 2H-β-beryllene, the
  spectral weight is redistributed even more strongly: the low-energy peak nearly vanishes, and
  a strong broad absorption peak appears around 6 eV to 6.5 eV. This substantial blue shift
  and enhancement suggest that hydrogen adsorption suppresses low-energy transitions and
  promotes higher-energy electronic excitations, likely through band-structure modification
  and the formation of new hybridized states involving hydrogen.

IV.     CONCLUSION


     In this work, we investigated the structural stability, electronic structure, optical re-
sponse, and topological properties of pristine and hydrogen-functionalized two-dimensional
beryllium phases using first-principles density functional theory calculations. Alongside the
previously reported α- and β-beryllene structures, we identified a dynamically stable cubic
trilayer beryllene phase. The phonon dispersion calculations also confirm the dynamical sta-
bility of 2H-α-beryllene, 1H-β-beryllene, and 2H-β-beryllene, while the molecular dynamics
simulations further support the finite-temperature stability of these hydrogen-functionalized
structures.
     Hydrogen functionalization substantially modifies the structural and electronic properties
of beryllene. The calculated adsorption energies indicate that the hydrogen-functionalized
structures are stable with respect to molecular hydrogen. In particular, 2H-α-beryllene
transforms from a metallic system into a wide-gap semiconductor, whereas the hydrogen-
functionalized β-based structures retain metallic behaviour.
     The calculated optical properties demonstrate that dimensional reduction and hydrogen
functionalization strongly modify the dielectric response of beryllium. Compared with the
bulk reference phases, cubic trilayer beryllene exhibits increased spectral weight at lower
excitation energies and enhanced absorption in the visible-to-near-ultraviolet region. Pris-
tine α- and β-beryllene show pronounced optical anisotropy and plasmon-related features.
Hydrogen functionalization further redistributes the optical spectral weight and shifts the
characteristic excitation energies through changes in electronic hybridization and screening.
     Parity analysis of the spin-orbit-coupled electronic states confirms the previously reported
non-trivial topology of α-beryllene and the trivial topology of β-beryllene. The cubic tri-
layer phase also has a non-trivial Z2 invariant. These results show that structural design
and hydrogen functionalization provide direct routes for tuning the electronic, optical, and
topological properties of two-dimensional beryllium.
